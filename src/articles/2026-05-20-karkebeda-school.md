In many parts of India, a school begins with a building, a bell, a timetable, and children walking through its gates. In Karkebeda, a remote village in Chhattisgarh’s Abujhmad region, a school began with a five-hour trek.

One February morning, education officials, guided by the village sarpanch and local residents, walked through narrow forest paths, climbed difficult terrain, and crossed streams without bridges or boats. Their destination was a village that had no road connectivity, did not appear on Google Maps, and until recently, was outside the formal reach of the state’s education system.

What they carried was simple: textbooks, slates, pencils, and a blackboard. What they brought was much larger: the first real promise of schooling for children who had waited years for the state to arrive.

### Conflict and Closure

Karkebeda lies about 70 km from the Narayanpur district headquarters, deep inside a region once known as a Maoist stronghold. Until 2012, children from the village could attend a primary school in Kongali, four kilometres away. But that school was shut after Maoist threats.

The new school in Karkebeda began in a temporary shed. Twenty children received books, slates and pencils. A local teacher, **Runita Pawe**, was appointed to teach them.
